My perspective

How I think about products.

Questions I get asked — and how I actually answer them.

You spec the guardrails, not the output. Define what failure looks like, how the product degrades gracefully, and what the human fallback is. Non-determinism is a design constraint, not an excuse. Forcing teams to define failure modes early surfaces assumptions that would otherwise blow up in production.

Transparency about what it can and can't do. Users don't need to understand transformers — they need to know when to trust the output and when to double-check. That's a UX problem as much as a model problem. Confidence thresholds, graceful fallbacks, and honest empty states matter more than accuracy on the happy path.

I can read a technical spec, spot unrealistic estimates, and have honest conversations about trade-offs without needing things translated. That builds trust with engineering teams quickly and means we can have the real conversation — not the polished one.

Both, and I think the distinction is starting to matter less. I learned product craft on regulated, high-stakes software before AI was a job title. That foundation — rigorous discovery, hard prioritisation calls, stakeholder alignment — makes my AI work more grounded, not less.

Less strategy decks than people think. Mostly: talking to users, unblocking the next decision, writing things down clearly so the team doesn't have to ask twice, protecting engineering from scope creep, and knowing which fires to let burn. The job is mostly context management and decision quality.
