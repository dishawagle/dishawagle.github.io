Context — what is a claims engine?

The 30-second primer

When a patient takes a prescription to a pharmacy, the pharmacist submits an electronic claim to the patient's insurance — asking: is this patient eligible, is this drug covered, and how much does the patient owe? The answer comes back in under a second. That answer — approve or reject, and at what price — is what a claims adjudication engine produces.

Get it wrong and a patient is denied medication they're entitled to, or told an incorrect reason why. Get it slow and the pharmacist's system times out. The patient, the pharmacist, the employer, and the finance team all have a stake in every response — and none of them should ever have to think about the engine that produced it.

This is the system I built.

Who I was building for

Four stakeholders, one primary user

The claims engine serves four groups simultaneously — but they don't all have equal weight. The patient is the primary user. They never interact with the engine directly — they just stand at a pharmacy counter waiting for an answer. Every product decision I made was ultimately tested against one question: does this serve the patient correctly, quickly, and without creating confusion they'll have to resolve through a phone call?

Patient — primary user

Right drug, right price, no friction at the counter. Never sees the engine — only experiences its output. A wrong rejection or an unclear reason code means a phone call to customer service, a delayed prescription, or an incorrect charge.

Pharmacist — direct integrator

Needs a fast, accurate response with a clear rejection reason they can explain to the patient. An NCPDP rejection code alone tells them what happened. An additional message tells them why — and what the patient can do next.

Employer — the buyer

Needs their employees to get lower drug costs and access to more pharmacies than they'd get through a single payor. This was the product promise that triggered the entire build.

Finance team — downstream consumer

Needs clean reconciliation at the end of every billing cycle. They feel the pain of pricing mismatches that patients never see. Their needs were real — but secondary to patient outcomes.

The problem

Why we built our own

Flipt relied on a single payor for drug pricing, pharmacy network coverage, and claims adjudication. Employers signing up were getting whatever that payor negotiated — not the best available price. That's a weak value proposition in a market where drug cost transparency is the entire product promise.

The product decision was to bring in multiple payors and route each claim to whoever offered the best deal for that patient and drug. To do that, Flipt needed to own the adjudication layer rather than rent it.

"We weren't just solving a technical dependency. We were making a strategic move — control our own pricing logic so we could offer employers genuinely competitive drug costs, not whatever one payor decided to negotiate."

The build decision — Flipt, 2019
The tradeoff we knowingly accepted

Building the engine didn't immediately eliminate the payor dependency. Claims still passed through the original payor for final processing — two systems calculating price independently. Drug prices update mid-week. Data syncs aren't always real-time. The result was a 15% pricing mismatch rate in reconciliation.

This was a known, accepted tradeoff — not a bug we failed to fix.

The tradeoff — patient savings vs. reconciliation simplicity
What we gave up Perfectly clean reconciliation. 15% of claims required finance team investigation because Flipt's price and the payor's price didn't agree.
What we gained Best available drug price for every patient on every claim. If another payor offered a lower price, the patient got it — always.
Why the mismatch was never fully resolved

The long-term answer was for Flipt to become a payor itself — winning on price through better contracts so the system's own pricing would increasingly be the lowest, reducing dependence on external payors. But as long as other payors could occasionally offer lower prices, we kept them in the network. Patient savings took priority over operational simplicity. The mismatch rate was managed through automated reconciliation checks, not eliminated.

PM work — discovery

How I defined what to build

The patient never gave me feedback directly. Neither did the pharmacist, in any structured way. My discovery process had to work through intermediaries — domain experts upstream who understood what the system needed to handle, and customer service downstream who heard what was going wrong after launch. Between those two feedback loops, I could define requirements before building and improve the product after shipping.

Discovery · Domain expert
Medicare plans — identifying the next product gap
A domain expert flagged that the engine only supported commercial insurance plans. Medicare plans — which cover tens of millions of Americans — have fundamentally different requirements and a different adjudication workflow. The formulary structure is different. The coverage rules are different. The patient population is different. We were leaving a major market segment entirely unserved.
Product outcome: Medicare expansion became a dedicated 12-month program — scoped from regulatory documentation, architected separately from the commercial engine, and delivered with its own test suite. I owned it as sole product owner.
Discovery · Requirements mapping
Translating clinical rules into system logic
Drug interaction checks, step therapy rules, prior authorisation workflows — none of these are intuitive to an engineer. I worked with pharmacists and benefits administrators to understand what each rule meant clinically, why it existed, and what the system needed to produce in response. Getting these wrong doesn't just mean a failed test — it means a patient is told they can't have a medication they need, or given a reason that doesn't help them resolve the problem.
Product outcome: Requirements were defined with domain experts before engineering began. Every check in the system traces back to a clinical or regulatory rule validated by someone who knew what it meant for the patient.
PM work — iteration

How feedback changed the product

After launch, the most valuable product feedback came through customer service — pharmacists calling in about confusing responses, patients being told incorrect rejection reasons, employers asking why reconciliation numbers didn't match. I sat in on customer service reviews to hear these directly, not filtered through a ticket system. That gave me the context to diagnose what was actually wrong and prioritise what to fix.

Iteration · Customer service feedback
Improving rejection messages — from system code to patient guidance
NCPDP defines standard rejection reason codes — a pharmacist seeing "refill too soon" knows what happened, but not what to tell the patient or what to do next. Customer service was fielding calls from pharmacists who needed more context to help patients at the counter. The fix was to add additional free-text messages to rejection responses — messages that explained the specific reason in plain language and gave the pharmacist something actionable to tell the patient. For example: a "refill too soon" rejection would now also include the exact date after which the refill would be eligible. The pharmacist could tell the patient directly, without anyone calling customer service.
Product outcome: Reduced pharmacist escalations to customer service. Patients got a clear answer at the counter rather than being told to call their insurance. We resolved 80% of high-priority patient issues within 7 days by keeping this feedback loop active throughout the product's life.
Iteration · Production incident
Wrong rejection code — diagnosed and patched live
A pharmacist called in because the rejection code didn't match the actual problem. The drug required prior authorisation — but the response indicated a formulary exclusion. A pharmacist can work with a PA rejection. A formulary exclusion means the drug isn't covered at all — a very different conversation with the patient. We pulled the claim from the audit log, traced the check sequence, identified the wrong code mapping, and patched it the same day. The pharmacist was called back with the correct guidance.
Product outcome: Corrected the prior auth rejection mapping. Added the case to the regression test suite to prevent recurrence. The incident confirmed the value of the customer service feedback loop — the bug was found because a pharmacist called, not because a monitor fired.
Technical depth — for those who want it

How the engine worked

Architecture

Built as a set of asynchronous microservices — some responsible for fetching data, others for validation and business logic. Services that were independent of each other ran in parallel to reduce latency. This was the architectural decision that made the sub-1 second SLA achievable under concurrent load. Sequential processing would have added hundreds of milliseconds to every claim unnecessarily.
